# Shrinkage-corrected histological compilation values average ~1.6× MRI-atlas values: an audit of a comparative neuroanatomy archive

Author: Atharva Chourey
Affiliation: Atharva Chourey Lab (independent research lab)

Manuscript in preparation — not yet peer-reviewed.

## Abstract

Comparative neuroanatomy rests on volumes measured by different methods in different decades, yet those volumes are routinely pooled as if they were commensurable. We audited a public comparative archive — the Brain Catalogue — and tested whether a scaling relationship fitted to its magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) segmentations could predict the published histological record. The audit covered 71 label files across 37 species: 53 files contained real data and 18 were empty placeholders with a correct grid and zero voxels. After forensic quality control, eight catalogue species supported a pre-registered log–log line relating hippocampus volume to brain volume. On the same lion specimen, the catalogue MRI label gave a hippocampus volume of 2.02 cm³, against 4.50 cm³ published by histological planimetry in Reep et al. (2007) — a gap of about 2.2× that boundary-sensitivity testing localised mainly to the histology side, not to delineation of the MRI labels. In a blind benchmark against 343 published species, pre-registered before the test values were examined, the MRI line failed its registered success criterion decisively: median absolute percentage error 66.0%, against 18.2% for the Patzke et al. (2015) model. Post-hoc decomposition, labelled as such in the underlying records, attributes the failure principally to a compilation-to-compilation ratio: for the species and regions actually paired, the shrinkage-corrected histological compilation values (Stephan/Baron/Reep, as tabulated by Patzke et al. 2015) average ~1.6× the MRI-atlas values at the same brain size, compounded by power-law form failure at small brain sizes. This ratio is a property of that named dataset pair, not a systematic property of histology as a method: raw (uncorrected) histological volumes run below MRI volumes, because histological processing shrinks tissue, and regional ratios reverse (see §1.1 and §4.4). Any comparison that pools the two compilations undeclared inherits the ratio for the paired material.

**Keywords:** comparative neuroanatomy; hippocampus; histology; MRI segmentation; allometry; data audit; pre-registration

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Two measurement traditions

Much of what is known about how brain structures scale across mammals comes from histology. In the serial-section tradition associated with Stephan and colleagues, and carried forward in compilations such as Patzke et al. (2015), brains were sectioned, structures were measured by planimetry on a sparse set of sections, one hemisphere was typically measured and doubled, and the resulting areas and volumes were corrected for tissue shrinkage incurred during fixation and embedding. Reep, Finlay and Darlington (2007), in "The Limbic System in Mammalian Brain Evolution," applied histological planimetry of this kind — 8–10 sections, one hemisphere measured and doubled, shrinkage-corrected — across a comparative sample, and that paper's Table 1 remains a primary source for limbic volumes, including the hippocampus.

A second tradition measures the same structures from magnetic resonance imaging (MRI): a scan is acquired, a structure is delineated on the image — by hand, by atlas propagation, or by a segmentation pipeline — and its volume is counted in voxels. MRI volumes need no embedding-shrinkage correction, but they depend on image contrast, on where a delineator places a boundary that histology may define differently, and on whether the specimen was scanned in situ or after extraction and long fixation.

Both traditions report a number in cubic centimetres. The shared unit invites pooling. Whether pooling is licensed is an empirical question, and it is the question this paper addresses. If two compilations disagree, then a scaling law fitted to one will mispredict the other by that disagreement, a cross-compilation headline ("species A exceeds species B") may reverse when the comparator changes compilation, and a synthesis that mixes compilations without declaration will encode compilation as if it were biology.

Three qualifications govern every offset statement in this paper, and they are stated here rather than left to the Discussion. First, the direction of any histology–MRI comparison depends on correction. Raw (uncorrected) histological volumes run *below* fresh and MRI volumes, because fixation, dehydration and embedding shrink tissue — substantially: Reep et al. (2007) report a mean volumetric shrinkage of 59.0% with correction factors of 2.1–2.9 in their material, and Wagstyl et al. (2020), working on the BigBrain human specimen, derive a volume-based shrinkage factor of 1.931. Only shrinkage-*corrected* compiled values can read above MRI values, and only because a correction multiplier has been applied. Second, the ratio is region-specific and can reverse. Nadkarni et al. (2019), comparing MRI against the Stephan et al. histological reference in the mouse lemur, found histology overestimating hippocampus size by about 38% relative to in vivo MRI while *underestimating* the pallidum by about 38% in the same comparison. No single factor describes histology across regions. Third, the ratio is compilation-specific: it depends on which correction factors, boundary definitions and estimation procedures a given compilation applied. Accordingly, this paper claims no systematic property of histology. Its offset claim, wherever it appears, is the rescoped one: a ratio between two named compilations — the shrinkage-corrected Stephan/Baron/Reep values as tabulated by Patzke et al. (2015), and MRI-atlas values — for the species and regions actually paired between them.

That the components of such a disagreement are known in principle is not in dispute. The underlying project's literature audit (see §4.4) verified 14 places in which a histology-versus-MRI difference, or its shrinkage-correction mechanism, is quantified or operationally applied — including direct cross-method quantifications, processing-shrinkage quantifications, and delineation studies showing that segmentation protocol alone can produce large volume differences. What had not been done, within that audit's coverage, was to grade hundreds of published compilation species against one MRI-fitted line and report the resulting dataset-pair ratio. This paper reports such a test, together with the archive audit that made it possible and the case studies that localise the disagreement for the paired felid material.

### 1.2 The archive

The Brain Catalogue (Santin, Herbin and Toro 2016) distributes post-mortem structural MRI scans of vertebrate brains, principally through Zenodo records under a CC BY-NC 4.0 licence. The lion scan used here, for example, is Zenodo record 44855: a single adult specimen — age and sex unstated in the record — at 0.3 mm isotropic resolution, N4 bias-field corrected. Crucially, the catalogue's Zenodo records do not contain per-species segmentation files. A further check of the catalogue's own website (braincatalogue.org), distinct from its Zenodo archive records, found that the project hosts per-species segmentation files that the Zenodo records omit. That recovery — finding labels on a project's website that its archive deposit lacks — is the starting point of the present work, and auditing what was recovered is its first result.

Label integrity matters for a simple reason. A file named `Hippocampus.nii.gz` that contains no voxels, or that contains a whole brain, or that contains a unilateral fragment, will silently corrupt any downstream volumetry that trusts filenames. In an archive assembled over years, exported at different times, and mirrored across a website and a repository, filename trust is not a method. Every label used here was therefore verified — by voxel census, by component structure, by header and affine consistency with its MRI, and by visual overlay against the scan — before any volume was computed from it. Section 3.1 reports what that verification found: a substantial fraction of the archive's label files are empty placeholders, and several of the non-empty files are artifacts of distinct, diagnosable kinds. The audit is not a preliminary embarrassment to be footnoted; it is a substantive finding about the state of a public comparative resource, and it determines the sample size of everything that follows.

### 1.3 Aims and design

This paper makes four linked contributions, each tied to a pre-registered or otherwise prospectively declared protocol in the underlying project records.

First, it reports a census audit of the catalogue's label files: how many files exist, how many contain real data, and what failure modes the remainder exhibit (§3.1).

Second, it reports a measurement that the audit recovered: the hippocampus volume of the catalogue lion specimen from its MRI label, set against the value published for the lion nearly two decades earlier by histology (§3.2). Two early summaries of this project claimed more than the record supports on this point; both claims are formally retracted in the underlying record and the retractions are carried into this paper (§3.2, §4.1).

Third, it reports a registered reconciliation test for the felid gap between the MRI labels and the histological values, designed to distinguish delineation error on the MRI side from inflation on the histology side (§3.3).

Fourth, it reports a blind benchmark: a scaling line fitted only to quality-controlled MRI catalogue labels, registered in advance, was graded without refitting against 343 published species drawn from Reep et al. (2007) and the Patzke et al. (2015) compilation (§3.4). The line failed its registered criterion. The failure, decomposed post-hoc and labelled as post-hoc throughout, yields the paper's headline finding in its rescoped form: the shrinkage-corrected histological compilation values average ~1.6× the MRI-atlas values for the paired species and regions — the "Two Rulers" of the underlying records, understood strictly as a compilation-to-compilation ratio (§1.1).

Two follow-up investigations complete the record and are reported with their final status, including where later registered tests superseded earlier explanations: a black rhinoceros residual that remains the record's one open anomaly (§3.5), and a ferret residual explained post-hoc as functional-form failure (§3.5). An attempted dolphin delineation that failed its registered quality gates, with no volume claimed, is reported in §3.6 as a record of a failed measurement, not rescued.

Throughout, we follow the underlying project's design rule: every number is either computed from a named scan, taken from a named publication, or explicitly recorded as unavailable. Missing data is never shown as zero, and method classes are never pooled undeclared.

## 2. Methods

### 2.1 Data sources

**Brain Catalogue scans and labels.** Post-mortem structural MRI scans and per-species segmentation files for 37 species were obtained from the Brain Catalogue: scans principally via its Zenodo deposits (CC BY-NC 4.0) and segmentation files via the project's website, after the website files were found to be absent from the Zenodo records. The lion specimen is Zenodo record 44855 (Santin, Herbin and Toro 2016), one adult specimen, age and sex unstated, 0.3 mm isotropic, N4-corrected. The black rhinoceros specimen was later confirmed, in a dedicated identity investigation, to be Zenodo record 44797, with the local MRI file byte-identical (md5-verified) to the deposit; its companion mesh is record 44795. Data are not redistributed with this paper; provenance, record numbers and checksums are held in the project's `data/*/PROVENANCE.md` files (see Data & Code Availability).

**Human newborn reference.** Where a newborn comparison enters the record (§3.4), it uses M-CRIB 2.0: 10 term neonates, postmenstrual age at scan 40.3–43.0 weeks, with manual 94-region parcellations, openly downloaded (OSF node `4vthr`). Newborn values are means ± standard deviation across the 10 subjects unless stated otherwise.

**Literature test sets.** The blind benchmark (§2.4) used two published sources, transcribed in full: Reep, Finlay and Darlington (2007), Table 1, and Patzke et al. (2015), Table 1 — the Stephan-series/Manger compilation, whose rows carry their underlying sources (Pirlot and Nelson 1978; Stephan et al. 1981; Baron et al. 1996; Reep et al. 2007; Montie et al. 2008; and new measurements in Patzke et al. 2015 itself). Additional literature values used for case studies are Bhagwandin, Haagensen and Manger (2017) for rhinoceros MRI volumetry, Sakai et al. (2016) for lion endocranial volumetry, and Jardim-Messeder et al. (2017) for lion dissection and isotropic-fractionator data. Bibliographic details are given in the References only where the underlying sources give them.

### 2.2 Label audit procedure

The audit proceeded in three passes, all recorded in the project's processing log and output tables.

*Inventory and census.* Every species page of the catalogue website was inventoried and the live site inventory was diffed against the locally held files; site and local holdings matched for all 37 species. Each label file was then subjected to a deep check: a file counts as containing real data only if it carries substantive non-zero content on a correct grid; a file with a correct grid and zero voxels (nz = 0) counts as an empty placeholder. Junk thresholds applied in the cross-species tables excluded files whose non-zero content was trivially small — for example a file with 1 non-zero voxel and a file with 272 non-zero voxels were both excluded as slivers, not structures — and the aquatic analysis states the working rule explicitly: a label is real only if non-zero voxels exceed 1,000. The sealed census result is reported in §3.1.

*Volumetry under per-file geometry.* Volumes were computed from each file's own header voxel dimensions (zooms), not from a single assumed resolution, because several files carry voxel-size metadata inconsistent with their MRI. Brain-volume denominators were taken from the catalogue's Atlas mask where present, otherwise from an Otsu-threshold mask computed from the MRI, with the source tagged per species in the output tables (`outputs/cross_species_volumes_v2.csv` and `outputs/cross_species_volumes_v3.csv`). The file named `Atlas.nii.gz` is a single-label whole-brain mask, not a multi-region parcellation, and was used only as a denominator. Two bulk downloads (the lion and zebra MRIs from the website) arrived as empty files; both species were already held from Zenodo and the Zenodo copies were used.

*Forensic quality control.* Each surviving hippocampus label was examined for component structure (number of connected components, share of the two largest components, left/right symmetry by centroid and, in follow-up forensics, by per-side volume), for header/affine identity with its MRI, and by visual overlay in three planes against the scan. Labels failing forensic QC were excluded from fits with reasons fixed in advance of the benchmark (see §2.3). The forensic table of record is `outputs/label_forensics.csv`. Overlay-verified values are flagged as such in the output tables; the lion hippocampus and telencephalon labels, for example, were verified on-grid (276 × 334 × 209 at 0.3 mm) with overlays anatomically correct.

### 2.3 The registered line and its fit set

A descriptive log–log fit over the ten initially verified catalogue hippocampus labels gave a scaling slope of 0.81 (r = 0.90) of hippocampus volume against brain volume — negative allometry: larger brains carry proportionally smaller hippocampi (§3.2). Forensic QC then removed two of the ten from the primary fit, with reasons registered in advance: the sloth bear label is an artifact (both main components lie on the same side of the midline; not a bilateral hippocampus), and the giraffe label is unilateral (its second component is 1.3% of the first). The resulting quality-controlled fit set contains eight species — black rhinoceros, cat, cheetah, ferret, giant panda, leopard, lion and red kangaroo — spanning brain volumes of approximately 6–443 cm³.

The primary line, pre-registered in `PREDICTION.md` (registration commit `d54361f`) before any literature test-set values were examined, is:

> log₁₀(hippocampus, cm³) = −1.6609 + 0.8467 × log₁₀(brain, cm³), n = 8, r = 0.930, p = 8.1×10⁻⁴.

A declared sensitivity fit — the unfiltered n = 10 headline line, slope 0.8089, intercept −1.5665, r = 0.897 — was registered alongside it so that the variant could not be cherry-picked later. The red kangaroo label's header metadata is corrupt (its zooms and origin were copied from the sloth bear files); refitting it at its own MRI's zooms moves the quality-controlled slope to ≈0.82, a variant likewise noted in the registration. Model-estimated points obtained by atlas propagation — catalogue human, chimpanzee, gorilla and grey wolf — never entered any fit set; they are reported, flagged, as a separate data class throughout.

Project rules, registered with the line, governed all subsequent tests: no refitting after a test set is seen (any revised line is a new dated registration); missing literature values are reported as missing, never imputed; and method classes (direct label / literature dissection / model estimate) are never pooled undeclared.

A registered point check accompanied the line. For the M-CRIB newborn (brain volume 374.4 cm³, measured hippocampus 2.90 ± 0.31 cm³, n = 10), the line predicts 3.30 cm³, a deviation of −12.0%. The check was registered as a confirmation target — the newborn sits modestly below the adult cross-species line — and proved stable across every fit variant tried (−11% to −15%).

### 2.4 The blind benchmark design

The benchmark protocol was pre-registered in `PREDICTION.md` §3 (commit `d54361f`) before any test-set value was extracted, with two declared exceptions: the Reep et al. lion and leopard hippocampus volumes, which had surfaced during a prior-art audit, were handled by the separate, non-blind felid reconciliation test (§2.5) and never by the blind benchmark.

*Eligibility.* A test species required a published hippocampus volume and had to be (a) not among the Brain Catalogue's 37 specimens and (b) not lion or leopard. 343 species qualified: 26 from Reep et al. (2007) Table 1 as the primary subset, and 317 further species from the Patzke et al. (2015) compilation.

*Operationalisations, declared in the benchmark record.* For Reep species, total brain volume is the sum of the row's 11 printed regional components (mm³ → cm³) — the paper's own accounting; the excluded lion row sums to 231.09 cm³, matching the ~231 cm³ figure stated when the test was designed. The paper's footnote states that the amygdala is also included in the paleocortex column, so the component sum double-counts the amygdala by ~0.2–0.7% of brain volume; the sum as printed was used unchanged. Hippocampus in Reep is the table's hippocampus column: it includes dentate gyrus, cornu ammonis, alveus, hippocampal commissure and fornix-fimbria caudal to the septum; it was measured on one side and doubled; and it is shrinkage-corrected — with species-specific factors for six taxa and a mean factor of 2.4 for the rest. For compilation species, brain volume is the table's Vb (ml), constructed by the compilation as brain mass / 1.036, and hippocampus is the table's Vh as printed. Species also present in Reep Table 1 were graded once, on the Reep primary values. Species with multiple specimen rows were averaged. Genus-level entries were graded as printed and flagged. One genus entry was excluded conservatively because catalogue overlap could not be ruled out.

*Transcription validation.* The compilation states Vb = Mb/1.036 for every row; all ~390 transcribed rows were checked against that identity. Exactly three rows fail beyond rounding — *Micropotamogale lamottei*, *Hipposideros sperois* and *Notopteris macdonaldi* — internal inconsistencies in the published table (dropped digits). Those three species were excluded and are reported here, not silently corrected. Separately, all 29 Reep hippocampus values agree between the Reep original and the compilation's corresponding rows to rounding (maximum discrepancy 0.005 cm³), so the two transcriptions corroborate each other.

*Comparators.* Two comparators were registered. The Patzke et al. (2015) model — the paper's best model, an exponential curve fitted to log-transformed data across its compilation, excluding cetaceans, elephants, hippopotami and the manatee from the fit — was gradeable and was graded as published: ln(Vh) = 9.26 × (0.72 − exp(−0.097 × ln(Vb))), with parameters a = 9.26, b = 0.72, c = 0.097, r² = 0.97. The natural-log reading was verified numerically against the paper's own table values across four orders of magnitude; a log₁₀ reading predicts impossible volumes and was rejected on that evidence. A material declaration accompanies this comparator: Patzke's model was fitted on essentially the benchmark's test set — its own compilation, including the Reep rows — so on 337 of the 343 test species the comparator is graded on its own training data, a large structural advantage. The benchmark reports a fully blind subset separately (§3.4) so that the verdict does not rest on in-sample rows. The second registered comparator, Karbowski (2007), proved not gradeable: its widely cited −0.14 exponent concerns volume-specific glucose metabolic rate, and the paper publishes no hippocampal volume equation at all. Nothing was reconstructed in its place, and the registered criterion was evaluated against the single gradeable comparator. That non-gradeability is itself reported as a finding.

*Metrics and criterion.* Primary metric: median absolute percentage error (APE) across the test set. Secondary metrics: mean signed percentage error (bias) and head-to-head win/loss against each gradeable comparator. The success criterion, declared in advance, was strict: the registered line counts as "predicting unseen anatomy better than existing models" only if its median APE is lower than every gradeable comparator's on the same species set. The scorecard was to be published in the project report regardless of outcome — and is, in §3.4, as a failure.

### 2.5 Felid reconciliation test

The felid test (`PREDICTION.md` §4) was non-blind by design, because the values at issue were already known: catalogue MRI labels — lion 2.022 cm³, leopard 1.178 cm³ — against Reep et al. histology — lion 4.496 cm³, leopard 3.121 cm³. The registered question was whether re-delineation or boundary placement on the MRI side could account for the ~2.2× gap.

Two tests were executed. Test A propagated the full hippocampal formation from the D99 macaque atlas onto the felid MRIs using the project's standard 1 mm affine pipeline. Test B measured boundary sensitivity of the catalogue labels themselves: each label was dilated by one voxel, and at an extreme bound by two voxels, inside the brain mask, and the fraction of the gap thereby closed was computed against registered midpoints (lion 3.26 cm³, leopard 2.15 cm³). Decision rules were fixed in advance: closure of ≥50% of the gap would indict delineation as the main driver; closure of <20% would exonerate the catalogue labels and point at the histological side (shrinkage-correction multiplier, sparse-section estimation, single-hemisphere doubling); 20–50% would be reported as mixed, claiming neither side.

### 2.6 Follow-up investigations: rhino, ferret, dolphin

Three follow-up builds were commissioned after the main record closed, each under its own pre-registration (`PREREGISTRATION_followups_2026-11-17.md`, commit `86c3636`; and, for the rhinoceros investigation, `PREREGISTRATION_rhino_anomaly_2026-11-17.md`, commit `e53edfd`), with the project rules carried over unchanged.

*Rhinoceros confirmatory re-delineation.* An initial post-hoc investigation had attributed the rhinoceros residual to a delineation-definition mismatch, and had recorded a fixed prediction in advance of any confirmatory run: re-delineating the catalogue rhinoceros MRI under one declared tight MRI protocol (criteria T1–T7, operationalising Bhagwandin et al. 2017) would give ≈4.2 cm³, on the registered line. Decision bands were fixed in advance: 3.57–4.83 cm³ would confirm the prediction; ≥5.48 cm³ would refute it; between would be indeterminate. Execution was a fresh delineation from the MRI, not a morphological erosion of the catalogue label, with hemispheres traced under identical rules and reported separately, and ±1-voxel boundary sensitivity reported.

*Rhinoceros five-direction investigation.* After the confirmatory test refuted its own prediction (§3.5), five further attacks were pre-registered: (1) specimen identity, by documentary chain; (2) whole-brain profile of the same specimen, against same-species MRI references, with a registered minimum of two referenced structures for any specimen-versus-structure verdict; (3) a second-specimen hunt across the catalogue census, Zenodo, MorphoSource, OpenNeuro, NITRC, post-2017 literature and the general web, with a "none openly available" verdict licensed only by a complete search log; (4) simulation of Bhagwandin et al.'s sampling/estimation scheme on the project's traced mask across every phase offset; and (5) line-side robustness — the residual restated against Patzke's model, against within-class ranks, and under a declared leave-one-out sensitivity, with the anomaly claimed only at the weakest formulation surviving all three.

*Ferret forensics.* The ferret residual was registered unexplained; any explanation is therefore post-hoc by rule. A forensic sequence was declared in advance — label integrity, denominator sensitivity, literature search, and functional-form assessment against the benchmark's size-band errors — which disciplines the investigation without changing its post-hoc status.

*Dolphin delineation.* Both dolphin hippocampus files in the catalogue are empty placeholders, so a delineation was attempted on the bottlenose MRI (0.30 mm; Atlas denominator 515.46 cm³) under a registered protocol: hippocampal-formation definition and landmarks from Montie et al. (2007, 2008), with cerebrospinal-fluid landmark calibration verified against the catalogue's own lion and cat labels. Five registered quality gates governed the attempt, including three-plane overlay inspection (the mask must form a bilateral lamina in the medial wall of the inferior horn, without spill or extension into lateral temporal neocortex), a component and dust criterion, a left/right ratio band, a pre-declared sanity band of 0.3–2.0 cm³ for this specimen, and ±1-voxel sensitivity reporting. Failure of any gate was to be recorded as failure, not rescued. The registered expectation, if the literature cetacean pattern held in the MRI class, was a residual of −70% to −95% against the registered line.

### 2.7 Aquatic census

A separate exploratory thread — post-hoc throughout, and labelled as such — inventoried the catalogue's aquatic and semi-aquatic material against the land set, using the project's pipeline unchanged and keeping method classes separate. Its census and measurement verdicts (§3.6) use the same label-reality rule as §2.2 and the registered line only as a fixed ruler, never refitted.


## 3. Results

### 3.1 Label recovery: 53 of 71 files contain real data

The sealed census of the catalogue's label files is the audit's first result. Of **71 label files** checked across the **37 species**, **53 contain real data** and **18 are empty placeholders** — files with the correct name and grid and zero voxels (nz = 0). The empty class is not marginal or confined to obscure structures. Roughly half of the `Hippocampus.nii.gz` files are empty placeholders, including those for the baboon, gorilla, chimpanzee, both macaques, the wolf and the catalogue's human specimen. Both dolphin hippocampus files are empty (§3.6). Any pipeline that had trusted filenames and treated empty files as zero volumes — or, worse, silently dropped them — would have manufactured a comparative dataset out of absences.

The non-empty remainder is not uniformly trustworthy either. The audit diagnosed distinct failure modes, each with its own signature:

- **Mislabelling.** The red squirrel's "Hippocampus" file duplicates its whole-brain mask. The file named `Atlas.nii.gz` is throughout a single-label brain mask, not a multi-region parcellation.
- **Junk placeholders.** The night monkey hippocampus file contains 1 non-zero voxel; the okapi hippocampus file contains 272 non-zero voxels — a sliver, not a structure. Both were excluded by name, with reasons, in the cross-species tables. The bottlenose dolphin's corpus callosum file (nz = 900) falls in the same junk class.
- **Partial labels.** The dolphin and chimpanzee "cerebellum" files are far too small to be the whole structure. The bottlenose cerebellum file, for example, measures 3.156 cm³ at its own header zooms (2.383 cm³ at the MRI's zooms — the two exports of this specimen carry inconsistent grids) and fills only ~2% of its own bounding box: a sparse trace, reported as a label artifact, with no cerebellar volume claimed.
- **Metadata inconsistency.** Several files carry voxel-size metadata inconsistent with their MRI. The red kangaroo hippocampus label's header zooms and origin were copied from the sloth bear files; its volume is 0.200 cm³ at its header zooms and 0.268 cm³ at its MRI's zooms, and every downstream quantity that uses it must declare which geometry was used. The slow loris and red squirrel headers carry 1.0 mm placeholder voxel sizes, destroying absolute scale in naive computations.
- **One-sided and unilateral labels.** Forensic component analysis (§3.2) later showed the sloth bear hippocampus label's two main components both lie on the same side of the midline, and the giraffe label to be effectively unilateral. Neither failure is visible from a voxel count alone; both required the component and symmetry forensics of §2.2.

Against that background, the positive recovery is equally concrete. Labels that passed verification include the lion hippocampus and telencephalon (§3.2) and a verified cross-species hippocampus set of ten species (§3.2). Two recovered labels illustrate that the archive's rarer label types also survive audit: the Neocortex label exists for exactly two species, and both files are real — ferret neocortex **1.87 cm³** and white-headed capuchin neocortex **27.33 cm³** (1.867 cm³ and 27.332 cm³ in the output tables). No aquatic species carries the Neocortex label type. A full 37-species sweep confirmed that the live website inventory and the locally held files match for every species: there are no further website files left unrecovered. To our knowledge, this is the first end-to-end, voxel-level audit of the region-label files of a public comparative-brain atlas archive (see SOURCES.md, claim P1b); prior published audits addressed mislabelling and orientation in a single human atlas, not empty delineations in a comparative archive.

The audit's consequence for everything downstream is a small, defensible sample. Ten hippocampus labels passed initial verification; forensic QC removed two (§2.3); eight species support the registered line. That reduction — from 37 species folders to an n = 8 fit — is not a weakness introduced by caution. It is what the archive, audited, actually contains for this structure.

### 3.2 The lion measurement, and the felid values

On the same lion specimen as the Zenodo scan (brain volume 188.1 cm³ by Otsu-threshold masking, sensitivity band 178–194 cm³), the catalogue's overlay-verified segmentations give:

- **Hippocampus: 2.02 cm³** (2.022 cm³ in the registered tables), **1.07% of the 188.1 cm³ brain**;
- **Telencephalon: 145.60 cm³ (77.4%)**, leaving a same-specimen non-telencephalic remainder of 42.5 cm³ (22.6%) — consistent with Sakai et al. (2016)'s hindbrain share of 20.7% of endocranial volume (n = 14).

To our knowledge, 2.02 cm³ is the first **MRI-based** hippocampus volume for the lion (*Panthera leo*) (see SOURCES.md, claim P1a). The qualifier is load-bearing, for the reason given next.

A legitimacy audit on 2026-11-17 found the prior art the project had initially missed: Reep, Finlay and Darlington (2007) published a lion hippocampus volume of **4,496.16 mm³ ≈ 4.50 cm³** (specimen 62-79) by histological planimetry, and a leopard value of **3.12 cm³** (3.121 cm³), nearly two decades before this project. Two claims in the project's early summaries are therefore formally retracted in the underlying record, and the retractions are repeated here so that no version of this paper circulates without them:

1. **"Lion hippocampus: first measurement in the literature" — retracted.** The catalogue value measured here (2.02 cm³) is the first MRI-based value only. The first published value is Reep et al.'s 4.50 cm³ (2007).
2. **The unqualified "newborn > lion hippocampus" headline — retired.** It is true only inside this project's MRI pipeline (newborn 2.90 ± 0.31 cm³ vs catalogue lion 2.02 cm³). Against Reep et al.'s published value the newborn is the smaller of the two (2.90 vs 4.50 cm³). Section 3.4 shows why the cross-compilation comparison was never licensed in the first place: the two compilations disagree for the paired material, so a headline that silently mixes them is not a measurement.

The verified cross-species hippocampus set, before forensic exclusions, comprises ten species (each volume from its own file's voxel size; denominator as tagged in `outputs/cross_species_volumes_v2.csv`): ferret 0.17 cm³ (2.8% of brain), cat 0.54 cm³ (0.9%), cheetah 0.85 cm³ (1.3%), red kangaroo 0.20 cm³ (0.7%), giant panda 1.07 cm³ (0.6%), leopard 1.18 cm³ (1.2%), lion 2.02 cm³ (1.1%), sloth bear 4.00 cm³ (2.1–2.7%), giraffe 2.92 cm³ (0.5%) and black rhinoceros 6.85 cm³ (1.6%). Percentages marked provisional in the source table, where the denominator is an Otsu mask rather than a catalogue Atlas mask, are not repeated as precise shares here. A descriptive log–log fit over these ten species gives a hippocampal scaling slope of **0.81 (r = 0.90)** against brain volume. The M-CRIB newborn (374.4 cm³ brain, 2.90 cm³ hippocampus) sits 11% below that descriptive line in the initial table — close to it: at birth, the human hippocampus is about the size the generic mammalian scaling predicts, marginally under. The registered, quality-controlled version of this analysis is §2.3 and §3.4.

Forensic QC of the ten labels (component census in `outputs/label_forensics.csv`) returned the following verdicts. The sloth bear label (+160% against the headline line) is a confirmed artifact: its top two components (84,565 and 24,570 voxels) both sit on the same side of the midline — not a bilateral hippocampus — and it was excluded. The giraffe label (−41%) is unilateral: top component 67,132 voxels, second component 878 voxels (1.3%); a bilateral-equivalent estimate of ~5.83 cm³ would sit near the line, but an estimate is not a label, and it was excluded. The black rhinoceros label is forensically clean (bilateral, top-two components 98.9% of the label) and its large positive deviation survived as a genuine residual (§3.5). The ferret label is forensically clean and its deviation likewise stood (§3.5). The felid labels are all forensically clean and sit tight on the line (cheetah +3.0%, leopard +5.4%, lion +7.8% against the headline fit; deviations against the registered n = 8 line differ by within ~2 points, as recorded in the aquatic report). The cat's negative deviation (−30.6%) carries a known denominator caveat: its Otsu brain volume (63.06 cm³) includes head tissue. The red kangaroo's deviation softens from −49.1% to −32% when its corrupt header is replaced by its MRI's zooms (§3.1). In short: the large outliers were mostly artifacts, and the audit — not biological storytelling — is what separated artifacts from residuals.

Atlas-propagated, model-estimated points were computed for four species whose catalogue hippocampus labels are empty, and are reported here strictly as a separate class, never pooled into any fit: human 6.85 cm³ (Harvard-Oxford propagation; QC overlay pass), chimpanzee 1.48 cm³ (D99 macaque-atlas propagation), gorilla 1.76 cm³ (D99 propagation; overlay sparse, flagged) and grey wolf 0.69 cm³ (D99 propagation across orders; overlay pass). All three primate propagations sit 30–50% below the catalogue-label line, consistent with affine-only propagation shrinkage rather than biology — which is precisely why they are excluded from fits. The chimpanzee point was initially flagged provisional pending an independent cross-check, blocked on a missing label key for the CIVM rhesus atlas. The key was subsequently found (hippocampus = label 79, 0.641 cm³ in the rhesus template; both earlier empirical candidates proved to be cortex). Propagating label 79 to the same chimpanzee scan through the standard pipeline gives **1.56 cm³** — +5.4% against the D99 value of 1.48 cm³ and −12% against a modern stereology value of 1.77 cm³ recorded in the project files. The provisional flag is therefore lifted: the chimpanzee point is corroborated as an affine-propagation estimate. The standing caveats are part of the result: both values are affine propagations, so their agreement cannot exclude a shared method bias, and the CIVM material is a foreign pipeline, used as cross-check material only.

### 3.3 Felid reconciliation: the gap localises mainly to the histology-compilation side

The felid gap, stated exactly, is: catalogue MRI labels lion 2.022 cm³ and leopard 1.178 cm³, against Reep et al. histological-compilation values lion 4.496 cm³ and leopard 3.121 cm³ — ratios of ~2.2× (lion) and ~2.6× (leopard; computed from the registered values) between the two compilations for the same two species. The registered test (§2.5) asked which side of that paired gap delineation could explain.

**Test A is graded VOID.** Propagating the full D99 hippocampal formation onto the felid MRIs failed as an instrument: Dice overlap against the catalogue labels 0.000 (lion) and 0.001 (leopard), with centroid offsets of 33.2 mm and 15.9 mm respectively. The cross-order primate-to-felid affine landed in the wrong territory. No rescue was attempted, and Test A contributes no evidence in either direction.

**Test B carried the verdict.** Dilating the catalogue labels by one voxel closes **15.5%** (lion) and **19.2%** (leopard) of the gap — inside the registered **<20% band** for both species. Even the extreme two-voxel bound reaches only 34.4% (lion) and 38.4% (leopard), inside the mixed band. Under the decision rules fixed in advance, the verdict is: **the catalogue labels are exonerated as the main explanation.** Delineation accounts for at most about a third of the gap, at an extreme bound that no plausible pen would adopt; the majority of the paired felid gap localises to the histological-compilation side — in the underlying record's terms, to Reep et al.'s shrinkage-correction multiplier (≈2.4 as a mean factor for taxa without species-specific factors, resting on estimated shrinkage of 51.8–65% as recorded in the project files; cf. the sweep-verified Table 2 range of correction factors 2.1–2.9, §4.4), sparse-section estimation over 8–10 sections, and single-hemisphere doubling.

Localising the gap is not proof of which exact step overshot, and this paper does not claim such proof. It is what the registered test could establish, and it is what it established: for these two paired species, a more generous MRI pen cannot manufacture the compilation values, so the difference must arise in how the compilation values were produced — most plausibly in a whole-brain shrinkage correction applied to a single structure, an assumption the prior literature itself flags as questionable (§4.4).

### 3.4 The blind benchmark: the registered line failed, and what the failure found

The registered line of §2.3 was graded blind against **343 published species** (Reep et al. Table 1, n = 26, plus the Patzke et al. 2015 compilation, n = 317; no Brain Catalogue species; lion and leopard excluded to §3.3). **It failed the registered success criterion decisively:**

| Full test set | 343 | **66.0%** | −53.5% | **18.2%** | +17.5% | 56–287 |

Full roster: `outputs/benchmark_testset.csv` and BENCHMARK.md Appendix A.*

Only **2.0%** of test species fall within ±25% of the line's predictions (18.7% within ±50%), against 61.2% within ±25% for the Patzke model. The failure is structural, not a matter of a few outliers. Two declarations accompany the scorecard and are part of the result. First, the Patzke comparator's in-sample advantage is real and large: it was fitted on 337 of the 343 test species. The verdict survives that advantage — the line also loses the Reep-only subset (55.5% vs 29.2%), loses the both-blind subset, and loses every size band (see below) — but the honest statement of what the benchmark shows is narrower than "our line is worse": it is that *a line fitted to eight MRI segmentations does not predict the shrinkage-corrected histological compilation record*. Grading Patzke's curve against MRI-segmentation values would be the symmetric test, and it has not been run. Second, the second registered comparator, Karbowski (2007), was not gradeable (§2.4); the criterion was evaluated against the single gradeable comparator, as registered.

The failure decomposes, post-hoc and labelled as such in BENCHMARK.md, into two components.

**Component 1: a compilation-to-compilation level difference (dominant inside the fit range).** The line underpredicts nearly everywhere in the compilation record: mean signed error −45.8% on the Reep subset, and −36.9% even restricting Reep to brains ≥20 cm³ (median APE 46.5% on that restriction). In the underlying records' post-hoc summary, the shrinkage-corrected compilation hippocampi are larger, at the same brain size, than the MRI-atlas hippocampi by a factor of **roughly 1.6** for the paired material, and ~2.2 in the felid case examined in depth in §3.3. Restated under the rescoping of §1.1 — and this restatement governs the rest of this paper — that factor is a ratio between two named compilations for actually paired species and regions, not a systematic property of histology. §3.3's boundary-sensitivity test already localised the felid instance of the ratio mainly to the compilation-production side. The benchmark generalises the felid observation to the dataset pair as a whole: an MRI-atlas allometry and the shrinkage-corrected compilation record are, for the paired material, two different rulers. Any future version of the line must either be fitted to, or explicitly bridged to, the compilation it claims to predict.

**Component 2: functional form at small sizes.** 247 of the 343 test species have brains under 2 cm³, two orders of magnitude below the registered fit range (≈6–443 cm³). There, true hippocampal share rises steeply — the benchmark record cites the least weasel at 8.1% of brain, the nine-banded armadillo at 11.0% and the common shrew at ~10.5% — the relationship bends, a single power law cannot follow the bend, and Patzke's saturating exponential, built for exactly this range, can. Size-band median APEs for the line are 65.7% (<2 cm³), 69.0% (2–20 cm³) and 51.3% (>20 cm³). The failure is not only extrapolation: inside the line's own fit range (5–450 cm³, n = 65) its median APE is still 62.6%, because Component 1 operates there too.

The known exceptions stay exceptional for both models. Cetaceans are the largest errors for the line (minke whale +1152%, harbour porpoise +575%, Atlantic white-sided dolphin +435%) and for Patzke's model (+652%, +706%, +343% respectively). For the elephant, the line errs by +168% and Patzke's model by +25% (the elephant is out-of-sample for both, since Patzke excluded elephants from their fit). Pinnipeds run the other way — the only Reep group the line overpredicts (California sea lion +44.9%, northern fur seal +27.1%, stellar sea lion +22.2%). The line's closest predictions are the harbour seal (+7.0%), squirrel monkey (−10.7%) and spider monkey (−18.5%); its worst, after the cetaceans and elephant, are the nine-banded armadillo (−85.7%) and the elephant shrews (−84.2% and −83.1%). The registered newborn point check (§2.3) resolved as registered: measured 2.90 cm³ against 3.30 cm³ predicted, −12.0%.

A by-product of the benchmark's transcription checks deserves recording, because it is the same species of finding as §3.1 at the level of a printed table: the identity Vb = Mb/1.036 caught **three dropped-digit errors in Patzke et al.'s published Table 1** (*Micropotamogale lamottei*, *Hipposideros sperois*, *Notopteris macdonaldi*). Those species were excluded from grading and are reported, not silently corrected. Published tables, like archive label files, reward verification and punish trust.

The registered line is accordingly retained only as a description of its own compilation class (MRI catalogue labels). The claim that it predicts the published compilation record is dead, and is recorded as dead here rather than quietly dropped. No refitting was done, and none is licensed by this report: any revised line — for example fitted across compilations with a declared bridge, or in a curved form — is a new hypothesis requiring its own registration and a fresh blind set.

### 3.5 Registered residuals: the rhinoceros stays open; the ferret is form failure

Two residuals against the registered line were registered unexplained in advance, with forensically clean labels: **black rhinoceros +80.5%** (measured 6.854 cm³ vs 3.80 cm³ predicted at brain 442.65 cm³) and **ferret +70.7%** (measured 0.173 cm³ vs 0.101 cm³ predicted at brain 6.13 cm³). (Deviations recomputed against later fit variants differ by small amounts — the rhinoceros appears as +82.8% and +83.5% in parts of the record; the registered figures are used here, with variants flagged where they matter.) Their final status differs, and the difference is instructive.

**Ferret: form failure (post-hoc verdict).** Forensic investigation under the declared sequence (§2.6) found the label clean — header and affine identical to the MRI, per-side volumes 0.0872 and 0.0860 cm³ (ratio 0.986, symmetric by volume), position verified on overlays in three planes — and the denominator exonerated: across the recomputed Otsu band (5.97–6.27 cm³) the residual stays at +67.7% to +74.8%, and a zero residual would require an 11.55 cm³ brain, which no published ferret value approaches. The compilation carries no *Mustela putorius* row, so no compilation value exists to compare; the least weasel, its congener in the Reep primary set, stands at +315.9% against the line. An independent MRI source (Hutchinson et al. 2017, as tabulated in a cross-species fractional-volume comparison in the project files) puts the ferret hippocampus at 3.63% of brain volume — above this specimen's 2.83%. Decisively, the line's demonstrated median error in the ferret's size band (2–20 cm³) is 69.0%: the ferret's +70.7% is a typical miss of the power-law form in that range, while Patzke's saturating exponential deviates the other way at the same brain size (−48.0%). Verdict: **form failure** — the power law's known small-brain under-prediction, measured on one of its own fit points. One catalogue-semantics note is recorded with it: the ferret Neocortex label overlaps 67.3% of the hippocampus label's voxels, because the catalogue's "Neocortex" file is the full cortical sheet drawn over the medial wall — a property of that label, not of the hippocampus label.

**Rhinoceros: reopened and unexplained — the record's one open anomaly.** The rhinoceros history must be told in order, because its earlier explanation was superseded by its own registered confirmatory test, and presenting the superseded explanation as standing would misstate the record.

A post-hoc investigation first attributed the residual to a delineation-definition mismatch. Its evidence included a same-specimen literature pair: Bhagwandin, Haagensen and Manger (2017) measured **4.71 cm³** by MRI volumetry on a black rhinoceros whose brain mass (531 g) matches the Patzke compilation row exactly — almost certainly the same specimen for which the compilation prints **7.48 cm³**, a **1.59×** ratio between the two published values for (almost certainly) one brain. Scaled to this project's specimen, the catalogue label sat +65% above a tight MRI-protocol expectation (≈4.16 cm³) but only +3.7% above a broad compilation-definition expectation (≈6.61 cm³). On that evidence the label looked like a broad-definition measurement living in an MRI dataset, and definition-matched, the rhinoceros sat near the line (+4 to +10%). A fixed prediction was recorded in advance: a fresh tight re-delineation would give ≈4.2 cm³.

The confirmatory test was then run under the registered tight protocol T1–T7 (§2.6). **Fresh delineation of the catalogue rhinoceros MRI = 6.97 cm³** (hemispheres 2.41 and 4.57 cm³; the catalogue label's asymmetry — 2.431 and 4.424 cm³ — reproduces under independent tracing), with ±1-voxel sensitivity −28.2%/+21.9% (erode-1 = 5.01 cm³). Against the fixed bands, the verdict is **REFUTED** (≥5.48 cm³): even a uniformly one-voxel-tighter tracing (5.01 cm³) stays above the confirmed band's ceiling (4.83 cm³). The declared exclusion classes (fimbria thread and dust) total only 1.5% of the label; the label's bulk is the MRI-visible lamina band, and that band measures ~7 cm³ under the tight criteria. The delineation-definition mechanism, as operationalised by its own registered test, therefore fails, and the rhinoceros residual **reopened** at +83.5% (tracing against the line), unexplained by denominator, exclusions, boundary placement or definition. The evidence inverts: two MRI tracings (catalogue 6.854 cm³, fresh 6.97 cm³) agree with each other and sit 1.09× below the compilation value (7.48 cm³), while Bhagwandin et al.'s 4.71 cm³ becomes the low outlier.

A five-direction investigation, pre-registered before any of its tests was run (§2.6), then attacked the reopened anomaly. (1) *Specimen identity: confirmed* — Zenodo record 44797, md5-identical file, *Diceros bicornis* named at every link; the catalogue specimen is documented to be a *different animal* from the Patzke/Bhagwandin specimen (531 g; catalogue mass-equivalent 458.6 g), so the "same specimen" inference applies only to the Patzke-row ↔ Bhagwandin pairing. (2) *Whole-brain profile: insufficient by the registered rule* — only two labels exist for this specimen; descriptively its hippocampal share is 1.548% (label) / 1.575% (tracing) against 0.919% in Bhagwandin et al.'s specimen, n = 1 against n = 1. (3) *Second specimen: none openly available* after the full registered search; the world's open rhinoceros brain MRI supply is this one specimen (MorphoSource/OpenNeuro searched via web index — a declared limitation). (4) *Bhagwandin reconciliation: fails* — the paper states no estimator, and every declared proxy simulated on the tight mask (2 mm slabs phase-median 7.21 cm³; Cavalieri 6 mm 6.92 cm³; ≥75%-fill 6.07 cm³) stays far from 4.71 cm³, retiring the partial-volume candidate by computation. (5) *Line-side robustness: the anomaly survives* — against Patzke's model the rhinoceros sits at +47.0% (label) / +49.5% (tracing), Patzke's own compilation value at +49.3% and Bhagwandin et al.'s at −6.0%; a leave-one-out sensitivity grows the line residual to +160–164%, so the in-sample figure is the conservative one; inside the MRI class the residual is the largest of any forensically valid direct label and its 1.548% share the largest of any valid large-brain direct label.

The restated anomaly, at the weakest strength surviving all five attacks, is therefore: a doubly measured MRI hippocampus of ~6.9 cm³ in a 442.65 cm³ black rhinoceros brain, extreme inside its own method class by every ruler available, and ~+50% above the saturating mammalian model from two independent compilations, for this species. Surviving hypotheses, ranked in the investigation record, are a genuine species or individual elevation; a rhinoceros-specific non-formation component inside the lamina band, namable only with histology that does not openly exist; and an individual anomaly of the world's only openly scanned rhinoceros. The deciding measurement — a second rhinoceros brain MRI delineated under T1–T7 — does not openly exist. No refit was taken at any stage.

### 3.6 The aquatic shelf and a failed delineation

The exploratory aquatic thread (§2.7) establishes how thin the catalogue's aquatic material is. Of the 37 species, exactly five are aquatic or semi-aquatic: bottlenose dolphin, La Plata dolphin, nursehound (a shark), platypus and Nile crocodile. There is no hippopotamus, no pinniped, no sirenian and no whale beyond the two dolphins in the catalogue at all, so the most obvious aquatic comparisons cannot be run from this dataset. Measurement yield is: one solid whole-brain volume (bottlenose, 515.46 cm³ by Atlas mask — notably about a third of the ~1,477 cm³ species-typical compilation brain volume, so this specimen is small, young or partial; the catalogue does not say), three usable telencephalon label volumes (La Plata 91.29 cm³, crocodile 12.21 cm³, nursehound 20.24 cm³, each reported without a share because their whole-brain denominators failed for diagnosed reasons), and **zero usable hippocampus labels**. Both dolphin hippocampus files are empty placeholders. The consequence is exact: **no MRI-label hippocampus volume exists for any aquatic species in this archive**, so no aquatic species can be placed on the registered line in the project's own method class. That placement is unavailable — not zero.

The literature exercise, kept strictly separate and never pooled with MRI labels, reproduces the known cetacean pattern against the registered line: the four literature cetaceans sit at residuals of −81.3% to −92.0% (Atlantic white-sided dolphin −81.3%, the one MRI-class literature point, from Montie et al. 2008; harbour porpoise −85.2%; bottlenose −90.0%; minke whale −92.0%). Because the shrinkage-corrected compilation values run above MRI-atlas values for paired material (§3.4), a compilation value already far below an MRI-fitted line only falls further under any compilation-to-MRI bridging — so the cetacean deviation survives the compilation correction in the direction that makes it stronger. The controls break any habitat reading: the manatee, a fully aquatic mammal of a different order, sits at +20.2%, and the hippopotamus — semi-aquatic and the cetaceans' closest living relative — at +47.1% and +5.6% for its two specimens. Four cetacean species sharing one common ancestor constitute effectively one evolutionary datapoint. What looks like a water story is a whale story: phylogeny, not habitat. No aquatic telencephalon share is computable (the bottlenose has a denominator but no telencephalon label; the other four have labels but failed denominators), and the source record states plainly that the coincidence hunt on proportions found nothing, for those stated reasons.

Finally, the attempted bottlenose delineation (§2.6) is reported as what it was: a failed measurement. The traced object measured 3.44 cm³ and **failed registered quality gates (a) and (d)**: sagittal and axial overlays show a rectangular slab of medial temporal (parahippocampal/perirhinal) cortex crossing cortical folds, not a lamina in the medial wall of the inferior horn, and 3.44 cm³ lies outside the registered 0.3–2.0 cm³ sanity band. Re-inspection concluded that in this extracted, formalin-fixed specimen the ventral and medial cerebrospinal-fluid borders that bound the formation in situ are collapsed, so the formation is not separable from parahippocampal cortex in this contrast. Montie et al. (2007), as recorded in the project files, state the same conclusion in print for this specimen class: the bottlenose dolphin is named among the species whose hippocampus could not be identified in formalin-fixed brain MRI, by exactly this mechanism. **No hippocampus volume is claimed for either dolphin** (the La Plata specimen received the same verdict on inspection). The registered expectation (residual −70% to −95%) is neither confirmed nor refuted; the test failed at the measurement stage and is recorded as failed, not rescued. What would make the measurement possible — an in-situ-style T2/PD scan, the catalogue team's own cetacean delineation, histology of the specimen, or an expert cetacean tracing — is listed in the underlying record and none of it is currently in hand.

## 4. Discussion

### 4.1 Which side the lion gap localises to — and what that does and does not prove

The felid result (§3.3) is the paper's most tightly registered finding, and its scope should be stated as tightly as it was earned. For two paired species (lion and leopard), with values in two named compilations, a one-voxel-more-generous MRI pen closes less than a fifth of the gap and an extreme two-voxel pen closes at most about a third. The majority of the gap therefore cannot live in MRI delineation. It localises to the production of the shrinkage-corrected compilation values: a shrinkage-correction multiplier of ≈2.4 applied as a mean where species-specific factors were unavailable, estimation from 8–10 sections, and doubling of a single measured hemisphere. Each of those steps is defensible in isolation; multiplied together, under a structure-blind whole-brain correction, they can plausibly overshoot for a single small structure — and the benchmark (§3.4) shows the resulting compilation reading above the MRI-atlas compilation across the paired record, not only in felids.

What this does not prove matters equally. It does not prove that any particular lion's "true" hippocampus is 2.02 cm³ rather than 4.50 cm³: the MRI specimen is a single, long-fixed ex vivo brain (fixation itself shrinks tissue, and the catalogue value carries no fixation correction), the histological value comes from a different individual (specimen 62-79), and no same-brain, both-methods measurement exists for the lion in this record. It does not prove that the compilation multiplier overshot by a specific amount for the lion. And it does not generalise, without further paired work, to structures other than the hippocampus or to compilations other than the two named here. The claim is a localisation, earned by a registered test with a void companion test honestly reported alongside it — nothing more, and nothing less.

The retractions of §3.2 belong in this discussion because they are the same lesson at the scale of a headline. An unqualified "first measurement" claim collapsed the moment a prior-art audit looked properly; an unqualified "newborn exceeds lion" headline was true in one compilation and false against the other. Both errors are compilation-blindness in different dress. The corrected claims — first *MRI-based* lion value, to our knowledge; newborn-vs-lion stated only within a named compilation — are weaker, and they are the ones the record supports.

### 4.2 Implications for mixed-compilation comparisons

The practical implication is a reporting rule, not a moratorium. Comparative volumetry will continue to need the historical compilation record — for most species it is the only record there is — and MRI atlases will continue to grow. What the benchmark forbids is silent pooling. Concretely, for the paired material studied here:

- **Declare the compilation of every value.** A hippocampus volume without its compilation (shrinkage-corrected histological compilation vs MRI-atlas compilation, and within those, which table and which delineation definition) is an underspecified measurement. The rhinoceros record shows why at single-specimen scale: one laboratory published 7.48 cm³ (compilation) and 4.71 cm³ (MRI) for almost certainly the same brain, a 1.59× ratio, without — in the underlying audit's full-text check — ever remarking on the discrepancy in the MRI paper itself.
- **Do not fit across compilations without a declared bridge.** A line fitted to MRI-atlas values mispredicts the shrinkage-corrected compilation by the dataset-pair ratio (~1.6× for the paired hippocampal material here) before any biology enters. A bridge factor, if used, must itself be estimated on paired material, declared, and allowed to vary by region — §4.4 shows regional ratios reverse, so a single global bridge would repeat the original error in corrected form.
- **Expect form failure to masquerade as method difference at small sizes, and vice versa.** Below ~20 cm³ brain volume, hippocampal share bends sharply and a power law fails regardless of compilation (Component 2, §3.4; the ferret, §3.5). Studies of small-brained taxa need a saturating or otherwise curved form, and method comparisons need to be made inside size bands, not across the full range at once.
- **Audit labels and tables before fitting to them.** Eighteen of 71 archive label files were empty; three rows of a canonical published table carried dropped-digit errors detectable by an internal identity. Neither fact required new data to discover — only verification. Verification passes of this kind are cheap relative to the cost of fitting to placeholders.

### 4.3 The rhinoceros anomaly as a scope marker

The rhinoceros (§3.5) is retained in this paper at length for a methodological reason: it marks the boundary of what compilation reasoning can explain. Its first explanation (definition mismatch) was plausible, evidence-backed — and refuted by its own pre-registered confirmatory test. Its five-direction follow-up retired two further candidate mechanisms by computation and documentation, and left a doubly measured, genuinely extreme value with no named mechanism and no second specimen in existence openly. Reporting that trajectory in full — including the superseded explanation, labelled superseded — is deliberate. A record that only publishes its surviving explanations teaches readers to trust explanations too much. The rhinoceros also enforces sample humility: it is n = 1, it was a member of the n = 8 fit set (so its headline residual is in-sample and, per the leave-one-out sensitivity, conservatively quoted), and no species-level claim about *Diceros bicornis* can be carried by one museum specimen, however carefully measured twice.

### 4.4 Prior art: what was already known, and what this paper adds

A precedent sweep and a literature audit accompany this paper's underlying record; their verdicts are adopted here and they rescope the paper's claims in three places. The full sweep record is held with the project papers; the audit of record is `outputs/two_rulers_literature_audit.md`.

*The offset's components were known.* The audit verified 14 items in which a histology–MRI difference or its shrinkage mechanism is quantified or operationally applied: 3 direct cross-method quantifications, 6 processing-shrinkage quantifications, 2 methods-authority statements and 3 delineation-class items. Among the direct quantifications, the closest prior work to the present study is Navarrete et al. (2018), who compared MRI measurements of 39 primate species against the Stephan et al. serial-section dataset (17 species with both) and found the hippocampus to carry the largest discrepancy of any structure examined (+31.5%, range 13.1–59.6, in the audit's reading of their percent-error convention, corresponding to a ratio of ≈1.3–1.46× depending on convention), attributing it to differential shrinkage and warning that datasets may be combined only while taking dataset differences into account. Delgado-González et al. (2015), measuring the same 12 human brains by ex vivo MRI and by histology, found histology above MRI for the hippocampus in all four hemisphere-by-age-group cells (pooled ratio ≈1.37×, computed in the audit from their published tables), attributed to clearer boundary identification in histology plus MRI partial-volume effects — delineation, not shrinkage correction, since their frozen sections carried no upward correction yet still read larger. Nadkarni et al. (2019) supply the essential counterweight adopted in §1.1: in the mouse lemur, histology overestimates hippocampus by about 38% against in vivo MRI and underestimates pallidum by about 38% — the ratio reverses by region. Shrinkage factors themselves are textbook-quantified across five decades: Reep et al. (2007) Table 2 reports 59.0% mean volumetric shrinkage with correction factors of 2.1–2.9; Wagstyl et al. (2020) derive a volume shrinkage factor of 1.931 for the BigBrain specimen (Amunts et al. 2013); and the stereology literature's standard guidance (Schmitz and Hof 2005, as reviewed in the audit) is that group comparisons should be restricted to tissue processed the same way. Within MRI alone, delineation criteria can spread hippocampal volumes widely — the audit records protocol comparisons producing up to 2.5-fold differences on the same scans (Boccardi et al. 2011, 2013) and a ~36% mean difference between two MRI datasets attributable to delineation criteria (Navarrete et al. 2019 erratum). Healy and Rowe (2007) warned, in general terms, that combining brain-size data produced by different methods without demonstrating appropriateness is a rigour failure that is rarely checked.

*What was not found, within the audits' coverage.* No statement of the composite compilation-to-compilation claim — the shrinkage-corrected comparative compilation reading ~1.6× the MRI-atlas compilation for the hippocampus across the paired record — was found in the audited literature, and no meta-analysis of the difference across hundreds of species was found. The largest direct paired quantification is Navarrete et al.'s 17 primate species. The present benchmark grades 343 species, an order of magnitude beyond that — with the caveat, stated in the audit and repeated here, that the present comparison is indirect (published values against a fitted line) where Navarrete's is paired (same structures, both methods). There is no standard name for the composite difference; "Two Rulers" is this project's coinage, and no equivalent term was found. One tension is stated in the audit and adopted here: the project's ~1.6× dataset-pair estimate is larger than Navarrete et al.'s paired hippocampal figure (≈1.3–1.46×). Plausible reasons, all consistent with the audited record, are that the present comparator is a fitted allometric line rather than paired measurements, that its taxa extend far beyond primates, and that the compilation's default correction (a mean factor of 2.4 for taxa without measured factors, per the project record on Reep et al. 2007) plausibly inflates the compilation side beyond specimen-specific corrections. The audits strengthen the phenomenon — and suggest this paper's point estimate sits at the high end of it. Finally, the sweep's verdicts on this paper's three novelty claims are: the first MRI-based lion hippocampus volume **holds** (P1a); the first end-to-end voxel-level audit of a comparative archive's region labels **holds** (P1b); and the ~1.6× offset claim is **narrowed** (P1c) to the compilation-to-compilation wording used throughout this paper. All three are listed, with their surviving wording, in SOURCES.md.

### 4.5 Limitations and scope caveats

These limitations are stated explicitly, as part of the results, not as boilerplate.

1. **Single specimens throughout.** Nearly every catalogue value and nearly every literature value is n = 1 per species. The sources publish no measurement uncertainty, so none could be carried through the benchmark. Species-level language in this paper always means specimen-level measurement, generalised no further than the text states.
2. **The registered line rests on n = 8.** Its fit range is ≈6–443 cm³ brain volume; 247 of its 343 benchmark species lie below 2 cm³, far outside that range. Its failure is therefore jointly a compilation result and an extrapolation result, decomposed in §3.4 but not fully separable with the data in hand. Inside its own fit range its median APE is still 62.6%, which is why the compilation component cannot be dismissed as pure extrapolation — but the n = 8 base remains small, and the line is retained only as a description of its own class.
3. **The comparator's in-sample advantage.** Patzke's model was fitted on 337 of the 343 test species. The benchmark's verdict survives on blind subsets, but the 66.0%-vs-18.2% headline must never be quoted without this declaration, and the symmetric test (Patzke's curve graded against MRI values) has not been run.
4. **The ~1.6× figure is a post-hoc decomposition, not a registered endpoint.** The registered endpoint was median APE, which failed. The ratio was computed afterwards to explain the failure, is an average over paired material with wide dispersion (felids ~2.2×; the rhinoceros single-brain pair 1.59×; paired primate literature ≈1.3–1.46×), reverses by region in the prior literature, and applies only to shrinkage-corrected compilation values — raw histological volumes run below MRI volumes. It must not be quoted as a constant of histology, a correction factor for general use, or a property of any species or region not actually paired.
5. **Ex vivo, long-fixed material.** The catalogue scans are post-mortem, extracted, in several cases long-fixed museum specimens. Fixation shrinkage and collapsed cerebrospinal-fluid spaces affect MRI values too — decisively in the dolphin case (§3.6) — and no fixation correction is applied to the MRI side in this record. The lion specimen's age and sex are unstated; the bottlenose specimen is far below species-typical brain size for reasons the catalogue does not give.
6. **Denominator heterogeneity.** Brain volumes come from catalogue Atlas masks where present and Otsu-threshold masks otherwise, tagged per species; some Otsu denominators are known-poisoned (the cat's includes head tissue) and four aquatic denominators failed outright (§3.6). Shares computed on mixed denominators are flagged provisional in the source tables and are not load-bearing in this paper's claims.
7. **Propagated points are a separate, weaker class.** Atlas-propagated hippocampi (human, chimpanzee, gorilla, wolf) are affine estimates with a demonstrated downward pull; they enter no fit, and the chimpanzee corroboration (§3.2) cannot exclude a bias shared by both propagation pipelines.
8. **Confounded comparisons inherited from the source project.** The newborn material enters only as a registered point check against the line; the underlying project's newborn-vs-lion framing varies species and developmental stage simultaneously, and no difference in that framing can be attributed to species alone. The aquatic analysis is exploratory and post-hoc throughout, and its cetacean cluster is effectively one phylogenetic datapoint (§3.6).
9. **Transcription and rounding bounds.** Compilation hippocampus values printed to 0.01 ml permit ±17–50% apparent error by rounding alone for the smallest species; both models' errors are inflated there, and the Reep subset (mm³ precision) shows the same ordering, so the verdict does not rest on rounding — but small-species percentage errors should be read with that bound in mind. Three published table rows were excluded as internally inconsistent (§3.4); no value was silently corrected anywhere in this work.
10. **Audit coverage.** The literature audit and precedent sweep were targeted, not exhaustive systematic reviews; every novelty statement in this paper is hedged to that coverage ("to our knowledge," "within the audit's coverage") and is listed in SOURCES.md for rescoping if further prior art surfaces. Verification levels vary by item and are flagged in the audit (some items were verified at abstract level or via detailed secondary summaries, as recorded there).
11. **Not measured / not reported in the underlying project records.** The following were not available to this paper and are not estimated anywhere in it: any lion amygdala, hypothalamus or periaqueductal grey volume (no published value exists in the record, and none was segmentable with validated tools on the single ex vivo scan); any lion diffusion or functional data (none exists openly; the specimen is post-mortem); a second rhinoceros brain MRI of any species (none openly exists, §3.5); a dolphin hippocampus volume in the MRI class (delineation failed at QC, §3.6); and per-specimen measurement uncertainties for the literature test set (not published by the sources). Where a number was needed and not in the records, this paper says so rather than supplying one.
